Your AI assistant remembers more than you think. Here’s how to audit it.

Dara Okafor · October 12, 2026 · 9 min read

Memory has quietly become the default in mainstream AI assistants rather than a feature you switch on. Most of them now build a persistent profile from your conversations, some of them can ingest context you upload, and a few will happily carry that profile across teams. This is genuinely useful — it is also the first time a consumer tool has kept notes about you that you never wrote and rarely review. A short, deliberate audit is the difference between a helpful assistant and an unmanaged record.

Practical principle: if you cannot see what your assistant remembers, you cannot decide whether it should. Review the memory before you trust the answers.

What memory actually is now

Three mechanisms are usually bundled under the word. The first is automatic extraction: the assistant notices a stated preference, a project name, a deadline, or a fact about your circumstances, and stores it as an item it can recall later. The second is manual or imported context: you paste in a profile, upload structured notes, or fill in custom instructions. The third is project-level knowledge, where documents attached to a workspace are treated as standing context.

All three are now mainstream. Assistants across the major platforms offer persistent memory, editable and deletable memory items, and a temporary or incognito mode that avoids retention. Several platforms added memory import during 2026, letting you load structured personal context once rather than repeating it conversation by conversation. Project-based knowledge bases on paid tiers can hold very large documents.

The practical consequence is that a conversation is no longer an isolated event. It is a training input for a profile that shapes every later answer.

Why inferred memory deserves more caution than your own notes

When you tell an assistant something, you know what it knows. When an assistant infers something, you often do not — and inferences are exactly the kind of memory that accumulates. “This user is dealing with a legal dispute”, “this user prefers to avoid this supplier”, or a guess about your role or seniority can all be written down as if you had stated them plainly, then quietly steer months of subsequent answers.

Three risks follow. Incorrect memory is self-reinforcing, because the assistant keeps acting on a wrong assumption and you may never see the note. Stale memory persists after circumstances change — the job you left, the address you moved from, the project that ended. And contextual memory can leak sideways: a fact stored while you were thinking about a personal matter can shape a work answer, and on shared or team accounts it may surface for colleagues.

Retention and training defaults also differ by platform and by plan tier. Some services exclude memory content from model training by default, others only on paid tiers or with an opt-out, and enterprise agreements change the picture again. Assume nothing; check the setting.

A ten-minute memory audit

Do this for every assistant you use more than occasionally, and diary it for a couple of times a year, because memory drifts as you use it.

  • Open the memory or personalisation settings. Find the list of stored memories, or ask the assistant directly what it remembers about you. Both routes should exist on a platform worth trusting.
  • Read every stored item. It takes minutes and is usually revealing. Delete anything wrong, anything stale, and anything you would not want read aloud in a meeting.
  • Check what was imported. If you uploaded a profile or context file, review it as a document. Imported context is often the largest and most sensitive block of memory in the account.
  • Confirm whether memory feeds model training. Find the data controls, note the default for your plan, and change it if you disagree with it.
  • Check project and workspace knowledge. Understand which documents sit in standing context, who else can see that workspace, and whether the assistant can cite them back to someone else.
  • Know the temporary mode, and use it deliberately. For a sensitive question, start a temporary or incognito conversation rather than relying on remembering to delete afterwards.
  • Test it once. Ask something only memory would answer — a preference you know you stated — to confirm the feature is behaving the way you assume.

What should never become a memory

Some content is fine to state and still worth keeping out of a persistent profile. Credentials and recovery codes, obviously. Account numbers, identity documents, and detailed financial positions. Health details and anything about another person that is theirs to share, not yours — client material, a colleague’s circumstances, a family member’s diagnosis. Anything covered by a confidentiality obligation or a non-disclosure agreement.

The habit that makes this manageable is the temporary mode, used on purpose rather than as an afterthought: if a question needs sensitive context, ask it somewhere nothing is retained. Redact what the task does not strictly require. And treat the assistant’s own conclusions about you as data you own and can edit, because on every serious platform it is exactly that.

If you use an assistant at work

Individual memory settings are only half the problem, because team and enterprise plans add shared projects, administrative controls, and retention policies that a user may not control. Three questions are worth settling with whoever owns the account: what does the platform store by default for our plan tier, who can see a conversation or project that one person created, and what is the retention and deletion process when an employee leaves or a project closes?

Where teams get into trouble is rarely the platform. It is an employee pasting a client contract into a personal account because the sanctioned tool was slower. Making the approved path fast and giving clear rules about what may never be pasted matters more than adding another policy document.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

In the United Kingdom and the European Union, memory content is personal data, which means you have the right to ask what is held about you, to have inaccuracies corrected, and in many cases to have it erased. Providers offer in-product controls for this, but a formal request is available if the in-product route fails.

In Canada, federal and provincial privacy law gives comparable access and correction rights for personal information held by commercial organisations, with the practical route being the provider’s privacy contact followed by the relevant commissioner.

In Australia, the privacy regulator has been pushing organisations to be able to explain automated decisions and to handle personal information transparently, and app-based AI features fall within that expectation. If an assistant has inferred something about you that shapes a service decision, you are entitled to ask how.

In the United States, several state privacy laws now give residents rights to access, correct, and delete personal data, and inferred profiles generally fall inside the definition. Enterprise customers in regulated sectors should expect these questions from auditors, and consumers can usually resolve them faster through the product settings than through a legal request.

The bottom line

Memory is what makes an assistant genuinely useful and it is not going away. The trade is straightforward: personalisation in exchange for a record you should occasionally read. Spend ten minutes reviewing what has been stored, put the sensitive questions in a temporary chat, and decide deliberately whether your conversations train anything. An assistant that remembers you on your terms is a better tool than one that remembers you by accident.

Questions readers ask

Can I turn memory off entirely?

Yes. Every major assistant offers a global memory or personalisation toggle, and most also offer a temporary or incognito conversation mode that avoids retention even when memory is switched on overall. Turning memory off reduces personalisation but does not necessarily change how the underlying conversation data is retained.

How do I find out what my assistant already remembers?

Open the memory or personalisation settings and read the list, or ask the assistant directly what it remembers about you. Both routes are standard. Reviewing it takes only minutes, and the practical benefit is catching stale or wrong inferences before they influence more answers.

Is memory used to train the models?

It depends on the platform, the plan tier, and the default in your region or account type. Some providers exclude memory content from training by default, others apply it only to certain tiers or require an opt-out. Check the data controls section of your account rather than assuming either way.
