AI Meeting Notetakers Are Useful. Check the Settings First.

Dara Okafor · September 24, 2026 · 9 min read

An AI notetaker that produces accurate summaries and action items is one of the few AI features that immediately justifies itself: fewer people typing, fewer forgotten commitments, a searchable record of decisions. It also creates something most organisations have never had — a complete, searchable transcript of every conversation, including the informal five minutes before the agenda starts. That archive is an asset and a liability, and which one it becomes depends on settings chosen in the first week.

What these tools actually capture

Most notetakers record audio, produce a full transcript with speaker labels, generate a summary and action items, and store all of it in a searchable archive tied to your calendar. Many also capture shared screens, chat messages, and attached files. Some join meetings as a visible participant; others operate from inside the meeting platform where attendees may not notice them at all.

The important detail is that the transcript, not the summary, is the product. Summaries are what people read; transcripts are what persists, gets indexed, and may later be searched by an administrator, disclosed in a dispute, or exposed in a breach. Treat the decision to enable one as a records-management decision rather than a productivity one, because that is what it is.

Consent is not just politeness

Recording rules vary by jurisdiction and, in some countries, by state or province: some places require only one participant to consent, others require everyone. With international calls the strictest applicable rule is the safe assumption. Beyond the legal position, undisclosed recording damages trust quickly, and people who suspect they are being recorded without notice say less — which removes much of the value you were buying.

The workable practice is simple and costs nothing: announce the notetaker in the invite, state it again in the first ten seconds, make the visible indicator obvious, and give anyone a straightforward way to ask for it to be stopped. For sensitive conversations — performance reviews, disciplinary matters, legal advice, health, redundancies, or negotiations — the default should be no recording at all.

Say it out loud at the start: what is being recorded, who can access it, and how long it is kept. Three sentences. Teams that do this consistently have far fewer awkward conversations later.

Settings to fix on day one

  • Retention. Set a default deletion window — 30 or 90 days suits most teams — rather than keeping everything forever.
  • Access. Decide whether transcripts are visible to attendees only, the whole organisation, or by request. Default to attendees.
  • Training. Turn off any option that allows your content to be used to improve the provider's models, unless you have a specific reason not to.
  • Auto-join. Disable automatic attendance at every calendar event; make joining a deliberate act.
  • External meetings. Require explicit confirmation before recording a call with people outside the organisation.
  • Storage location. Check which country the data sits in, especially if client contracts specify residency.

Then check who is an administrator. Full-text search across everything the organisation has said is a significant capability, and it should sit with a named, small group with a documented reason to hold it.

Accuracy, and where it fails

Transcription quality in good conditions is high, but accuracy drops with strong accents, overlapping speech, poor microphones, technical vocabulary, proper nouns, and numbers. Summaries introduce a second layer of risk: they compress, and compression involves judgement. A summary can confidently attribute a decision to the wrong person or state an action item nobody agreed to.

The mitigation is procedural. Treat the summary as a draft, have the meeting owner scan and correct it before circulating, and never let an AI-generated action item become a commitment without a human confirming it. Keep names, figures, dates, and anything contractual under particular scrutiny — those are exactly the fields that get quietly mangled.

Getting real value from the archive

The productivity gain comes from what happens after the meeting, not during it. Push action items into the system where work actually lives rather than leaving them in the notetaker, and keep the meeting record as the source for “what did we decide” rather than “what must I do”. A summary that nobody converts into tasks is a tidier version of notes nobody reads.

It is also worth using the tool to hold fewer meetings. Recordings and summaries make it reasonable for people to skip a call they do not need to attend live, provided the summary is trusted and circulated promptly. Teams that pair a notetaker with an explicit “you may decline and read the notes” norm get more from it than teams that simply record the same crowded calendar.

Regional notes

United States

Recording consent requirements differ by state, with several requiring all parties to agree. For multi-state or international calls, announce and obtain consent from everyone — it is the only practical way to stay on the right side of the strictest rule in the room.

Canada

Federal and provincial privacy rules govern the collection of personal information, including recordings of employees and customers. Notify participants, document the purpose, and confirm whether your provider stores data in Canada if that is a contractual requirement.

United Kingdom

Recordings containing identifiable people are personal data, which brings obligations around purpose, retention, and access requests. Individuals can ask for a copy of what is held about them, so an unmanaged archive of transcripts creates real administrative work — short retention windows help.

Australia

Surveillance and listening device legislation varies by state and can apply to private conversations, so consent practices should follow the strictest applicable state rule. Privacy obligations around employee and customer information apply to transcripts as they do to any other record.

Questions readers ask

Do I need everyone's consent to use an AI notetaker?

In some jurisdictions yes, in others only one participant's. With mixed or international attendance, assume the strictest rule applies: announce the recording in the invite and again at the start, and honour requests to stop.

How long should meeting transcripts be kept?

Most teams are well served by a default of 30 to 90 days, with deliberate retention for specific records that need to last longer. Indefinite retention creates search, disclosure, and breach exposure for very little practical benefit.

Are AI meeting summaries accurate enough to rely on?

They are good drafts, not records. Accuracy falls with overlapping speech, accents, technical terms, and numbers, and summaries can misattribute decisions. The meeting owner should review and correct before circulating.

Should notetakers be used for sensitive meetings?

No. Performance reviews, disciplinary or legal discussions, health matters, and negotiations should default to no recording. The value of candour in those conversations outweighs the convenience of a transcript.

The bottom line

AI notetakers pay for themselves quickly, but they quietly create a searchable archive of everything your organisation says. Announce them, set a short retention window, turn off model training and auto-join, limit administrative search, treat summaries as drafts, and keep sensitive conversations off the record entirely. Configure it in week one and the tool stays an asset.
