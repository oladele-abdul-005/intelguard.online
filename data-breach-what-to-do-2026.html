What to do in the first 24 hours after a data breach notice

Tomas Berg · October 11, 2026 · 8 min read

A breach notice arrives with a familiar set of feelings: irritation, a vague sense of exposure, and the urge to do something immediately. The problem is that the notice itself is often the most confusing part. It may come from a company you barely remember using, it may be written to protect the sender, and it may be genuine or a phishing attempt wearing the same clothes. The first day matters, but not because you need to act in a panic. It matters because the order in which you do things determines how much of the damage you actually prevent.

Practical principle: the breach notice is the start of a process, not a single event. Confirm it, secure what it exposed, and then watch the months that follow.

First, confirm the notice is real

Breach notifications are a favourite disguise for scammers. Before clicking anything, close the message and navigate to the company’s website yourself, using an address you type or a bookmark you already trust. Real notices rarely require you to act within hours, and they should not ask for your password, a payment, or a one-time code.

Look for the details that make a notice credible: which service, which time period, what categories of data, and what the company says it is doing next. If the message is vague about all four, treat it as suspect. When in doubt, contact the company through a phone number or support channel you looked up independently, not one printed in the message.

Change the password that matters most

Start with the account that can reset all the others: your primary email. If someone controls your email, they can walk into almost everything else through password resets. Change that password first, then change the password for the breached service, then work through any account that shares the same password.

This is the moment to stop reusing passwords. If a shared password leaked anywhere, it is effectively public. A password manager makes the cleanup finite: generate a unique password for each important account once, and the next breach becomes a single change instead of a weekend project.

Turn on the strongest second factor available

A password alone is weak against stolen credentials. The order of strength is straightforward: a hardware security key or passkey is best, an authenticator app is solid, and SMS codes are the fallback of last resort because phone numbers can be hijacked through SIM swapping.

Enable the strongest option your important accounts support — email, banking, cloud storage, social media, and work tools first. Save the backup codes somewhere that is not the account itself and not a note on the same device, then remove any old phone numbers or devices you no longer use from the trusted list.

Check what the breach actually exposed

Not all breaches deserve the same response. A leaked email address is a nuisance that leads to more spam. A leaked password is a credential problem. A leaked government identity number, full card details, or answers to common security questions is an identity problem that can outlive the breach itself.

Read the notice for the specific data categories, and write down the date and what was exposed. That record helps later if you need to dispute a fraudulent charge or explain a credit inquiry, and it prevents the vague anxiety that comes from not knowing what is actually at risk.

Match the response to the data

For passwords, the response is a change plus strong second-factor authentication. For payment cards, it is checking recent statements and replacing the card if the issuer recommends it. For identity numbers, it is a credit freeze or a fraud alert with the major credit bureaus, and in some countries a report to the national identity or fraud authority.

Where the breached organisation offers free monitoring, use it as one signal among several — it catches some kinds of fraud and misses others. A freeze or alert is stronger because it restricts new accounts from being opened in your name rather than merely reporting them afterwards.

Watch for the slow follow-up attacks

The most dangerous phase usually comes weeks later. Attackers combine stolen details with public information to build a convincing message: your real name, your employer, a genuine order number, a reference to a service you use. That is why the standard advice to look for spelling mistakes no longer works. Judge messages by what they ask you to do, not by how polished they are.

Two attacks deserve particular attention. SIM swapping, where someone convinces a carrier to move your number to their device, defeats SMS codes — so switch important accounts to an app or a key. And spear phishing aimed at your workplace can use a personal breach as the opening line. Tell your employer if work details were exposed, and expect unexpected contact to increase.

Keep a record and follow the provider’s steps

Save the notification email, note the date and the exposed data categories, and keep a simple log of anything you change. If the company provides specific steps, such as resetting a linked account or re-enrolling in multi-factor authentication, follow them in order and confirm each one took effect by signing out and back in.

Finally, do not let the cleanup end at the breached service. Search your password manager for the same password and retire it everywhere. The breach is a reminder about your whole account map, not just one login.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

In the United States, credit freezes with the three major bureaus are free and can be lifted temporarily when you apply for credit. State attorneys general and the Federal Trade Commission accept identity theft reports that create a paper trail.

In Canada, the two national credit bureaus offer freezes or alerts, and the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre takes reports. Provincial privacy regulators may also be interested when a company mishandles your data.

In the United Kingdom, the Information Commissioner’s Office and Action Fraud are the main reporting routes, and organisations must notify affected people when a breach poses a risk to their rights and freedoms.

In Australia, report to Scamwatch and the Australian Cyber Security Centre, and consider a credit ban with the national credit reporting bodies. In all four countries, a report to the police may be needed if fraud has already occurred.

A first-24-hours breach checklist

  • Confirm the notice by navigating to the company yourself — never through the message’s links.
  • Change your primary email password first, then the breached service, then any reused passwords.
  • Turn on the strongest second factor available and save backup codes offline.
  • Record what data was exposed and the date of the notice.
  • Freeze or alert your credit if identity or financial data was involved.
  • Replace SMS codes on important accounts where an app or key is supported.
  • Expect more personal, better-written scams in the following weeks — verify everything.

The bottom line

After a breach notice, the right response is orderly rather than dramatic: verify the message, secure the account that can unlock the rest, add strong second-factor authentication, and match your action to what was actually exposed. Then stay mildly suspicious for a few months. Most of the harm from a breach is prevented by boring steps taken early, not by elaborate ones taken late.

Questions readers ask

Should I freeze my credit after every breach notification?

No. Match the response to what was exposed. A leaked email address or password calls for a password change and better second-factor authentication. A leaked government identity number or full financial details is the case where a credit freeze or similar protective measure is usually worth the effort.

The notice says my password was not exposed. Am I safe?

Not necessarily. Even without a password, stolen names, addresses, phone numbers, and purchase history are enough for convincing targeted phishing. Expect more personalised scams in the following weeks and verify unexpected contact through a channel you choose yourself.

How long should I keep watching after a breach?

Treat the first 30 days as the high-risk window and the first year as the period for periodic checks. Malicious use of stolen data is often slow, so keep the notification, set a reminder to review your accounts, and stay alert to new-account or credit alerts.
