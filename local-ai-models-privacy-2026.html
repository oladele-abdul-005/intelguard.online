Local AI models: a private option when the cloud is too much

Dara Okafor · October 11, 2026 · 9 min read

Every prompt sent to a cloud AI service leaves your device, travels through someone else’s infrastructure, and lands in a system governed by terms you probably did not read. For a recipe idea, that is fine. For a contract, a medical letter, a client’s unpublished work, or anything covered by a confidentiality obligation, it deserves a second thought. Local AI models — the kind that run on your own laptop — exist precisely for that second thought. They are not as capable as the largest cloud systems, but in 2026 they are good enough for a surprising amount of everyday work.

Practical principle: choose the smallest tool that does the job. If the task does not need the world’s biggest model, it does not need to leave your machine.

What local AI is good at — and what it is not

Local models shine at bounded, text-heavy tasks: summarising a long document, rewriting a paragraph, extracting action items from notes, drafting a routine email, translating a page, or answering questions about a file you have loaded. They are also excellent for transcription and for tidying up dictated notes, where the value comes from convenience rather than deep reasoning.

They are weaker at tasks that need broad world knowledge, current information, long multi-step reasoning, or heavy code generation. A local model will happily produce a confident answer that is out of date or subtly wrong. Treat it as a competent assistant with no internet access and an unreliable memory, and plan your use accordingly.

Hardware: the honest requirements

Running a model locally is mostly a memory problem. A small quantised model — the kind that fits in a few gigabytes — will run on a modern laptop with 8 GB of memory, though you will feel it when other applications compete for space. A mid-size model is comfortable at 16 GB. Larger, noticeably smarter models want 32 GB or a graphics card with plenty of video memory.

Two practical notes. First, quantisation — compressing a model to use less memory — trades a little quality for a lot of practicality, and for most everyday tasks the trade is invisible. Second, an older laptop can still participate: a machine that struggles with video editing may run a small model perfectly well, because the workload is steady rather than bursty.

Choosing a model size that fits

Start smaller than you think you need. Install a small model, use it for a week on real work, and note where it fails. If the failures are about style or format, a bigger model may help. If the failures are about facts, a bigger local model may not help at all — that is a job for a search-backed cloud tool.

Keep at least two models on hand: a fast small one for quick cleanups and a slower larger one for careful drafting. Most local AI applications let you switch between them in a single click, and the difference in waiting time is far more noticeable than the difference in prose quality for short tasks.

Privacy is a spectrum, not a switch

“Local” describes where the model runs, not automatically what the application does. Before trusting a tool with sensitive material, check four things: whether it sends anonymous usage telemetry by default, whether it can fall back to a cloud model when it is unsure, where it stores your conversation history, and whether it downloads anything beyond the model files themselves.

Then apply the same discipline you would use anywhere else. Redact names and account numbers you do not need to share, keep the source documents on an encrypted disk, and treat the model’s output as a draft that a human reviews before it goes anywhere. Local processing removes the network risk; it does not remove the need for judgement.

Practical uses worth setting up first

Three uses repay the setup time almost immediately. Transcription: recording a meeting or voice memo and having it converted locally means the audio never leaves the machine. Document summarising: pointing a model at a long PDF and asking for the ten decisions inside is faster than skimming and more reliable than memory. And drafting: the first ugly version of an email, a policy note, or a product description, produced instantly and edited by you.

Add files and folders to the workflow gradually. A local assistant that can search your own notes is genuinely useful, but an index of everything you own is also a single place where a mistake can expose far more than you intended. Start with one project folder, not your entire drive.

When the cloud is still the right answer

Use a cloud service when the task needs the most capable model, up-to-date information, large-context analysis, or image and video understanding. The top cloud models remain meaningfully better at difficult reasoning and at work that depends on knowing what happened recently. There is no prize for doing badly locally what a cloud tool does well.

A sensible split: run the routine, private, high-volume tasks locally, and reserve the cloud for the hard questions. That combination keeps most of your text on your own hardware, keeps your subscription bill lower, and preserves access to frontier capability when you actually need it.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

In the United States, state privacy laws increasingly distinguish between data you disclose to a service and data processed on your own device. Local processing is the simplest way to stay out of that discussion, and hardware for it is widely available.

In Canada, where several provinces have their own privacy statutes, keeping client material on local hardware can simplify compliance conversations. Check whether your professional obligations require any particular storage controls.

In the United Kingdom, the information commissioner’s guidance on AI and data protection favours data minimisation and transparency — both easier to demonstrate when sensitive text never leaves the device.

In Australia, unreliable connections in some regions make local models genuinely practical rather than merely private: a tool that works offline does not care about the weather or the nearest tower.

A local-AI starter checklist

  • Size the model to your memory, not to a benchmark leaderboard.
  • Start with a small model and upgrade only when a task fails for a size-related reason.
  • Check telemetry, cloud fallback, and history settings before loading sensitive files.
  • Keep a fast small model for cleanups and a slower one for careful drafting.
  • Index one project folder first, not entire drives.
  • Redact what the task does not require, and review every output before it leaves your hands.
  • Keep a cloud tool for hard reasoning, current facts, and large-context work.

The bottom line

Local AI is no longer a hobbyist’s compromise. On ordinary modern hardware, it handles summarising, drafting, transcription, and document questions without sending your words anywhere. It will not replace the most capable cloud models, and it does not need to. Used for the tasks that suit it, it quietly removes a large slice of everyday work from other people’s servers — which is the point.

Questions readers ask

How much memory do I need to run a useful local AI model?

As a rough guide, a small quantised model runs in about 8 GB of system memory, a mid-size one is comfortable at 16 GB, and larger models benefit from 32 GB or a dedicated graphics card with plenty of video memory. Quality increases with size, but so does your hardware bill.

Is a local model truly private?

It is private by default in the sense that your prompts stay on your machine, but check what the application does next. Some tools send optional telemetry, download updates, or fall back to a cloud service for hard questions. Read the settings before you paste anything sensitive.

Should I replace my paid AI subscription with a local model?

Not necessarily. Local models are excellent for summarising, drafting, transcription, and working with private documents. The largest cloud models still lead on complex reasoning, current information, and long conversations, so many people use both.
