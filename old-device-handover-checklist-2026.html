Before you sell or recycle an old phone: the handover checklist most people skip

Tomas Berg · October 21, 2026 · 12 min read

Almost everyone does the obvious part. They back up the photos, tap erase, and hand over a phone that looks new. The obvious part is also the part that matters least, because on a current phone the wipe is automatic and thorough. What is not automatic is everything that still points at you after the screen goes back to its welcome page: the device sitting in your account list, the car key that lived in its wallet, the camera app that still streams to it, and the number attached to a plan you forgot you were paying for.

Practical principle: sign out first, wipe second, and keep a written record of both.

What a factory reset actually does

On any phone shipped in roughly the last ten years, the storage is encrypted by default. A factory reset does not carefully overwrite your holiday photos with zeroes; it destroys the key that makes that data readable. The files are, in a real sense, still there for a few seconds and then they are nothing. This is why the old advice of running three passes over a drive, or buying software that promises a "military-grade wipe", is not useful on a modern handset.

The corollary is worth saying out loud: the reset is one small step, and it is the step that handles the thing people worry about most. It says nothing about the accounts configured on the device, the lock that those accounts impose, or anything the phone was a key to. Some laptops are the exception worth checking. A machine with a traditional spinning drive, or an older tablet that predates default encryption, genuinely does need its storage overwritten or physically destroyed rather than simply erased.

The unlink list: what still points at you after the wipe

Before you erase anything, spend ten minutes on the list of links that survive it. Each of these is a reason a buyer ends up with a paperweight, or a reason a stranger ends up closer to your accounts than you intended.

The account itself comes first. If the phone is still signed in to your store account when it is erased, most platforms deliberately re-arm a lock on reboot, because a wiped device that cannot prove who owned it looks exactly like a stolen one. From your side that lock is an inconvenience; from the new owner's side it is a dead phone. Then there is the device list: many services treat a recognised phone as a second factor, and removing the hardware without removing it from the list leaves an entry that can still approve sign-ins, or at least a device you will be notified about for months.

The wallet is the one people forget. Payment cards, transit passes, hotel and office keys, garage and car keys, and in some places an identity credential all live in a secure element that a factory reset may or may not clear cleanly. Remove them from the wallet app before you erase, not after. Paired accessories have the same problem in miniature: a watch, earbuds, hearing aids or a camera that still considers the sold handset its controller is a device you handed over twice.

Two more are easy to miss. Work and school devices carry a management profile that only the organisation that issued it can remove cleanly, and messaging apps often keep active sessions on a laptop or tablet that survive the phone being wiped. Log out of linked devices where the option exists rather than assuming one erase covers everything.

The handover checklist, in order

Order matters more than effort here. Sign out before you erase, and everything after the wipe is just paperwork.

  • Get the data off, then check it landed. Photos, messages, notes, authenticator accounts if you use them, and any two-factor recovery codes stored in the device. Confirm they exist somewhere you can open today, not in a backup you have never tested.
  • Remove the wallet contents by hand. Cards, passes, transit, and any car, home, office, or hotel key. Do this from the wallet settings rather than trusting the erase, and let the device finish syncing the removal while it is still online.
  • Sign out of the store account. This is the step that releases the lock. Sign out first, confirm the account is gone from the device, and only then start thinking about erasing. If the option appears, remove this device from your account's device list as well.
  • Release the number and the plan. Eject the SIM tray if there is one. On eSIM-only hardware, delete the plan from the settings, or ask your carrier to detach it from the device. If you are keeping the number, make sure it has moved to the new phone first.
  • Unpair the satellites. Watches, earbuds, fitness bands, hearing aids, cameras, smart home hubs and anything that treats the old phone as its controller. If the phone was itself a hub or a monitor for a doorbell camera, log that app out too.
  • Strip the work or school profile. Do not try to force this. Ask IT or the school to release the enrolment, or remove it through the management app they gave you, then let the device complete its own managed wipe.
  • Erase, and confirm from the setup screen. Run the reset, choose a full or secure erase if the device offers one, and let it finish on a charger. When it reboots to the language selection screen you are done; do not sign back in to "just check", because that re-adds the account you just removed.
  • Write the record down. Date, make and model, IMEI or serial, the fact that you signed out and erased, and who took the device. A trade-in confirmation email does this for you. A private sale deserves a one-line receipt, because the only good time to prove you handed the thing over is before anything goes wrong.

Selling to a stranger is not the same as handing it to your kid

A family handover feels safe, which is precisely why it goes wrong. The right move is to treat your child or relative as a new owner: erase, then let them set up from a fresh account. A phone handed over while still signed in to you will keep receiving your mail, your photos, your prompts to approve someone else's login, and sometimes your number. The same applies in reverse if you pass a phone down and keep the line: whoever holds that SIM can now receive your codes, which is a much shorter path to your accounts than any phishing email.

With a stranger, the temptation is to be helpful. Do not log back in to demonstrate that the phone works, and do not hand over a device with your gallery, email, or banking app open "so you can see it is clean". The setup screen is proof that it works and that it is yours no longer. If a buyer insists on seeing your account activity to trust the device, that is information you should be paying them for, not the other way round. For the meeting itself, keep the transaction and the conversation in the marketplace rather than moving to a private messaging thread, and pick somewhere with people and cameras.

If the phone is dead, locked, or you cannot sign in

A device that will not boot is still an open link. The storage is a brick to anyone who finds it, but the accounts on the other side of it are still attached to you, and you can close them from anywhere: the provider's website, or your other phone, will let you remove a device you can no longer touch. Do the account work first, then deal with the hardware. It is a reasonable trade-in in either case, and an even more reasonable recycling drop-off.

If you are locked out of your own account, the recovery route on the account itself is the only one worth your time, even when it takes days and asks for documents. Paid third-party "unlock" services are mostly a way to lose money, and occasionally a way to lose your serial number to someone else's use. And if a device genuinely cannot be signed out of its lock, say so plainly when you sell it: a buyer who knows they are taking a risk can price it, and one who finds out later cannot be blamed for being annoyed.

Getting rid of it for good: recyclers, trade-ins and what to ask

Trade-in programmes from carriers and manufacturers are the least worrying path, because they expect a signed-out device and their pricing collapses if you have not signed out. Independent resale shops are more variable. Two questions sort them quickly: what happens to the storage before it is resold or exported, and will you get a written record that the device was received. A shop that handles this properly will answer both without hesitation.

For recycling, look for a certified processor rather than the nearest bin. In North America that means a recycler accredited under R2v3 or e-Stewards; in the United Kingdom it means an approved treatment facility working under the waste electrical and electronic equipment rules. Ask whether they destroy or overwrite storage, and whether they can confirm what happens to items they export for parts. Two practical details apply everywhere: anything with a lithium battery should never go in the household bin or a curbside cart, and if you drop a phone in a collection point, do it after the wipe and sign-out, not instead of them.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

In the United States, there is no single national rule for household electronics waste, so the practical picture is state by state. A majority of states restrict or ban some electronics from the ordinary trash, California being the oldest and one of the strictest, and several have added screens and handheld devices to what counts as covered equipment. Big-box retailer take-back and carrier trade-in programmes are the usual route. One thing to check before you sell a phone that is more than a few years old: if you set it up while it held a state digital ID, a digital driver's licence or a mobile key for your car, remove those from the wallet app specifically, because they do not always travel with the rest of the account.

In Canada, e-waste handling is largely provincial, with regulated product-stewardship programmes in British Columbia, Quebec, Ontario and several other provinces running collection networks and depot drop-offs. The retail route is straightforward, and national carriers run trade-in and take-back programmes that will accept devices that no longer work. If you are trading in, keep the confirmation email: it is the receipt that shows the device left you on a specific date.

In the United Kingdom, the waste electrical and electronic equipment rules put the duty to fund and provide collection on producers, with a take-back obligation on larger sellers for like-for-like items, which is what makes the retailer or delivery pickup a legitimate route rather than a favour. Your local council household waste site will take phones and batteries. On the theft side, the used-handset market has tightened: several marketplaces and resale chains now ask for ID and a receipt from private sellers, and some areas run activation-lock or "no proof, no purchase" schemes aimed at stolen phones. That is a reason to keep your own proof of purchase when you sell, not just when you buy.

In Australia, handsets and chargers sit in an industry-run take-back scheme with drop-off points through the postal network, and most councils run e-waste collections a few times a year; the national scheme for televisions and computers covers the rest of the household. Phones with a wallet, a transit card or a state digital licence are increasingly common there, so the same advice applies: clear the wallet app by hand, then erase. If you are selling privately, the buyer will usually want to check the IMEI against a stolen-device register, so having your own record of the serial number and the sale makes the exchange shorter for both of you.

The bottom line

The wipe is the easy half and it is already done for you by the encryption in the device. The work is the unlinking: sign out of the account so the next owner can use it, clear the wallet so nothing you rely on is sitting in a stranger's pocket, release the number, unpair the accessories, and put the whole thing in writing. Do that in order and an old phone stops being a key to your accounts and becomes what you actually meant to sell: a screen, a battery, and about forty minutes of someone else's setup.

Questions readers ask

Is a factory reset enough to make an old phone safe to sell?

For the data on the device, usually yes. Phones from the last decade encrypt their storage, and erasing destroys the key, so the leftover blocks are not readable by ordinary means. What a reset does not do is remove the device from your accounts, clear an activation lock, or take payment cards and digital keys out of its wallet. Do those steps first, then erase.

Do I have to remove the eSIM if I am wiping the phone anyway?

Treat the plan as a separate step from the wipe. A physical SIM used to handle this for you because you simply took the card out; an eSIM has no card to pull, and the plan can stay associated with the device profile until you delete it or your carrier detaches it. If you cannot remove it before handover, tell the carrier the device has left you and confirm the number is no longer attached to it.

I already sold it and forgot to sign out. What now?

Work from your other devices and the provider websites: remove the sold device from your account's device list, sign it out remotely where the option exists, and take any wallet cards out of the account. If the buyer is now facing an activation lock you created by not signing out, you are usually the only person who can release it, which is worth doing quickly for both of you. Change the password for any account you can no longer confirm was signed out, and be wary of paid third-party unlock services.
