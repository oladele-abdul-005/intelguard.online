How to protect your online accounts: the short answer

The best way to protect your online accounts is to combine several safeguards: use a different long password or passkey for every important service, turn on multifactor authentication, keep devices and apps updated, learn to recognise phishing, protect your email account first, and prepare secure recovery options before you need them.

That combination matters because account attacks have changed. Criminals do not always need to “hack” a complicated system. They can buy or steal an old password, send a convincing message, trick someone into approving a login, infect a device with an information-stealing program, or take control of a phone number. Once inside an email, cloud, banking, shopping, or social media account, they can reset other passwords and impersonate the victim.

This guide explains the practical steps individuals, families, freelancers, students, and small businesses can take in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, Europe, Asia, and Africa. The tools and account menus vary by country and provider, but the underlying security principles are the same.

Why account security is more difficult in 2026

Your digital identity is spread across dozens of services. Email, online banking, mobile money, social networks, cloud storage, work platforms, shopping accounts, health portals, government services, and messaging apps may all contain valuable information or connect to another account.

Attackers exploit that connected ecosystem in several ways:

  • Credential stuffing: a username and password stolen from one site are automatically tested on many others.
  • Password spraying: attackers try a small number of common passwords against many accounts.
  • Phishing and smishing: fake emails, websites, text messages, QR codes, or social messages persuade people to reveal credentials.
  • Adversary-in-the-middle attacks: a fake login page relays information between the victim and the real service in real time.
  • MFA fatigue: an attacker repeatedly sends login approval prompts until a tired or confused user accepts one.
  • Infostealer malware: malicious software collects saved passwords, browser cookies, cryptocurrency wallets, and session information.
  • SIM swapping: social engineering at a mobile carrier allows an attacker to move a victim’s number to a new SIM.
  • Account recovery abuse: weak recovery email addresses, old phone numbers, or poorly protected support channels become the easiest way in.

The lesson is simple: account security is not one setting. It is a chain. If the password, device, email account, recovery method, and login approval are all protected, an attacker has far fewer opportunities.

1. Protect your email account first

Your main email account is usually the master key to your digital life. If an attacker controls it, they may request password resets for social media, shopping, cloud storage, and other services. They may also search old messages for invoices, identity documents, travel details, or financial information.

Start with your email provider’s security dashboard. Review recent sign-ins, connected devices, active sessions, forwarding rules, recovery addresses, phone numbers, and third-party applications. Remove anything you do not recognise. If you see suspicious activity, change the password from a trusted device, sign out of other sessions, and enable the strongest available MFA method.

Check email forwarding and filtering rules specifically. Attackers sometimes create a hidden rule that forwards security alerts to an address they control or moves warning messages into a less visible folder. Also review your “sent” folder and deleted messages for evidence of unauthorised activity.

For a work or small-business email account, administrators should protect privileged users first. Require stronger authentication for administrators, finance staff, executives, help-desk personnel, and anyone with access to customer data. A compromised administrator account can turn one stolen login into a company-wide incident.

2. Use a password manager and unique passwords

Never reuse the same password for important accounts. If one service suffers a breach, reused credentials can be tested against your other accounts within seconds.

A password manager makes unique passwords practical. It can generate long, random passwords, store them securely, fill them only on the correct website, and warn you about reused or exposed credentials. Choose a reputable password manager with strong encryption, secure account recovery, support for MFA or passkeys, and applications for the devices you actually use.

Your password manager’s master password is especially important. Make it long, unique, and difficult to guess. Do not use a password that already appears on another website. Protect the manager itself with a passkey, hardware security key, or authenticator app where supported.

Modern password advice is more useful when it focuses on length and uniqueness rather than forcing people to create a short, confusing mixture of symbols. Do not change every password on an arbitrary schedule if there is no sign of compromise; change it immediately when a service reports a breach, when you entered it into a suspicious site, or when an unfamiliar login appears.

Prioritise these accounts first:

  1. Primary email
  2. Banking, payments, and mobile money
  3. Password manager
  4. Work and school accounts
  5. Cloud storage
  6. Government and health portals
  7. Social media and messaging
  8. Online shopping and delivery accounts

3. Turn on multifactor authentication everywhere possible

Multifactor authentication, or MFA, asks for more than one type of proof before allowing access. That proof might be something you know, such as a password; something you have, such as a security key or phone; or something you are, such as a fingerprint or face scan.

MFA is one of the most effective improvements most people can make. If a password is stolen, the attacker still needs the second factor. CISA recommends MFA for email, financial services, file sharing, and other sensitive systems, while also warning that not every MFA method offers the same level of protection.

Use the strongest option available:

  • Passkeys or FIDO2 security keys: best protection against remote phishing because the cryptographic credential is tied to the legitimate website or app.
  • Authenticator-app codes: generally stronger than SMS, but a real-time phishing site may still trick a user into entering the code.
  • Number-matching push approvals: better than simple “approve” prompts because they reduce accidental approvals, but users still need to reject unexpected requests.
  • SMS or voice codes: better than no MFA, but vulnerable to phishing, SIM swapping, and weaknesses in mobile signalling systems.

Never approve an unexpected MFA request. If prompts keep appearing, change your password from a trusted device, revoke active sessions, and contact the provider through its official website or application.

4. Consider passkeys for your most important accounts

Passkeys are replacing passwords for supported websites and apps. They use public-key cryptography: the service stores a public key while the private part remains protected by your device, password manager, or security key. You typically sign in with a fingerprint, face scan, device PIN, or screen lock.

The most important benefit is phishing resistance. A passkey is created for a specific website or service, so a fake website cannot simply collect a reusable password. Google describes passkeys as an easier alternative to passwords and says they are designed to resist phishing and credential stuffing.

Passkeys are not magic. Protect the device and cloud account that stores or synchronises them. Register a backup passkey or security key for critical accounts, and understand the provider’s recovery process before removing your old login methods. For highly sensitive administrator accounts, a device-bound passkey or physical FIDO2 security key may be preferable to a synchronised credential, depending on the organisation’s risk model.

When a service offers a passkey, check that you are on the genuine website or official app before creating one. Do not create credentials after following an unexpected email or text link.

5. Learn to spot modern phishing

Phishing is no longer limited to an obviously fake email full of spelling mistakes. Messages may use correct branding, familiar logos, realistic language, compromised business accounts, QR codes, short links, or AI-generated text. An attacker may begin with an email, follow up by text, and then call while pretending to be a bank, courier, employer, colleague, or technical-support agent.

Pause whenever a message creates urgency. Common warning signs include:

  • A demand to verify an account immediately
  • A warning that a payment, delivery, or payroll action will be cancelled
  • A request for a password, one-time code, recovery phrase, or MFA approval
  • A link whose domain is slightly different from the real one
  • A QR code that takes you to a login page
  • A request to install remote-access software
  • A message asking you to keep the conversation secret
  • A change to payment details or a request for gift cards or cryptocurrency

Do not use the contact details or link supplied in a suspicious message. Open the official app manually, type the known website address, or use a trusted phone number from a statement or the back of a bank card. For work payments or account changes, confirm through a second channel with the person you already know.

If you clicked a suspicious link but did not enter information, close it and run a security check. If you entered a password, change it immediately from a trusted device and change it anywhere else that password was reused. If you entered banking information or sent money, contact the financial institution’s fraud team as soon as possible.

6. Keep your phone, computer, browser, and apps updated

Software updates often fix vulnerabilities that attackers are actively using. Turn on automatic updates for your operating system, browser, password manager, messaging apps, and security software. Restart devices when required so the update actually finishes.

Remove applications you no longer use, especially those with access to contacts, files, accessibility controls, notifications, location, or screen capture. Review browser extensions and delete unfamiliar ones. Install applications only from official stores or the vendor’s verified website. Be cautious with pirated software, unofficial app packages, cracked games, and unknown browser add-ons; they are common delivery routes for information-stealing malware.

Use a screen lock, device encryption, and remote-find or remote-wipe features. Do not leave an unlocked phone or laptop unattended in a public place. A stolen device can be as useful to an attacker as a stolen password if sessions remain active.

7. Protect against SIM swaps and phone-based attacks

A phone number is useful for communication, but it should not be your only protection for sensitive accounts. Ask your mobile provider whether it offers an account PIN, port-out lock, SIM-change alert, or number-transfer protection. The exact feature name varies between carriers and countries.

If your phone suddenly loses network service without explanation, treat it as a possible security event. Contact the carrier through an official number. Check your email and financial accounts from another trusted device, and ask your bank to watch for unauthorised activity.

Avoid posting unnecessary personal details publicly. Birthdays, addresses, pet names, employers, school names, and family information can help attackers pass weak identity checks or personalise a scam.

8. Secure account recovery before you need it

Recovery is part of authentication. A strong password and MFA can be undermined by a weak recovery email, an old phone number, or a support process that accepts easily guessed information.

Use a recovery email account that is itself protected by a unique password and strong MFA. Keep backup codes in a secure offline location, such as an encrypted vault or printed copy stored safely at home. Do not keep the only recovery code inside the account you are trying to recover.

Register more than one secure authenticator for critical accounts where the provider allows it. Test the recovery process occasionally, but do not remove a working method until the backup has been verified. Be suspicious of anyone who claims to be support and asks for a password, MFA code, recovery phrase, or remote access.

For organisations, document an account-recovery process that includes identity verification, approval separation, logging, and a way to disable a compromised session. Help-desk impersonation is a real attack path, so “the caller knows personal information” should not be considered sufficient proof.

9. Review your accounts monthly

Set a recurring monthly reminder for a short security review. Check:

  • Recent login activity and unfamiliar devices
  • Active sessions and connected applications
  • Password reuse and exposed-password alerts
  • MFA methods and recovery information
  • Email forwarding rules
  • Mobile and browser permissions
  • Software-update status
  • Cloud files and sharing links
  • Financial transactions and saved payment methods

Delete old accounts you no longer need. Every forgotten account is another place where an old password, personal detail, or payment method may remain exposed. If deletion is impossible, remove stored payment details, change the password to a unique random value, and revoke unnecessary access.

Regional considerations for global readers

The security advice is broadly universal, but the services and risks differ by location.

United States and Canada: use official bank fraud numbers, credit-monitoring or identity-restoration services where appropriate, and government reporting portals when you experience identity theft or fraud. Businesses should consider state, provincial, and federal privacy obligations when handling customer data.

United Kingdom and Europe: be cautious with fake delivery messages, tax refunds, parcel fees, and bank alerts. Organisations should align identity and breach-response practices with applicable UK GDPR, EU GDPR, NIS2, and sector-specific requirements; legal obligations differ by country and business type.

Australia: protect banking, myGov, superannuation, health, and telecommunications accounts with strong MFA and unique credentials. Treat unexpected messages about tolls, parcels, tax, or account verification as potential scams.

Asia and Africa: mobile-money, SIM-swap, messaging, marketplace, and social-engineering scams deserve particular attention. Never share a mobile-money PIN, one-time code, or banking approval with a caller. Use official carrier and financial-service channels, and remember that a message appearing to come from a familiar contact may come from a compromised account.

These examples are not a substitute for local legal or financial advice. The safest general rule is to navigate directly to the official service rather than using a link in an unsolicited message.

What to do if an account has already been compromised

Act quickly, but do not panic.

  1. Use a trusted device and change the compromised password.
  2. Change it anywhere else it was reused.
  3. Sign out of all sessions and revoke unknown applications.
  4. Replace suspicious MFA and recovery methods.
  5. Check email forwarding rules and recent activity.
  6. Contact your bank, mobile carrier, employer, or platform through an official channel.
  7. Scan the affected device and update its software.
  8. Preserve suspicious messages, login alerts, receipts, and timestamps.
  9. Warn contacts if messages may have been sent from your account.
  10. Report fraud to the relevant platform, financial institution, carrier, or national reporting authority.

If an attacker has installed malware, changing passwords on the same infected device may not be enough. Use a clean device or reset the affected device after preserving important files safely.

The practical account-security checklist

For the fastest improvement, complete these steps today:

  • Protect your primary email with a unique password and strong MFA.
  • Use a password manager.
  • Replace reused passwords on banking, email, work, and social accounts.
  • Add a passkey or FIDO2 security key where supported.
  • Turn on MFA for every important account.
  • Stop approving unexpected login prompts.
  • Enable automatic updates.
  • Add a carrier account PIN or port-out protection.
  • Save backup codes securely.
  • Review sign-in activity and connected apps once a month.

No single tool prevents every attack. But these habits make common attacks—stolen-password reuse, basic phishing, password spraying, and unauthorised recovery—much harder to succeed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the safest way to protect an online account?

Use a unique long password or passkey, enable the strongest MFA available, keep your devices updated, and secure your recovery methods. Protect your email account first because it can reset many other accounts.

Are passkeys safer than passwords?

Passkeys are generally more resistant to phishing and credential stuffing because they use cryptographic credentials tied to the legitimate service. You should still protect the device, password manager, and recovery process that give access to the passkey.

Is SMS two-factor authentication safe?

SMS MFA is better than no MFA, but it is weaker than passkeys, hardware security keys, and usually authenticator apps. SMS codes may be exposed through phishing, SIM swapping, or mobile-network weaknesses, so use a stronger method when available.

Should I change my password every 90 days?

Change a password when it is exposed, reused, entered into a suspicious website, or connected to an unauthorised login. Forced frequent changes can encourage predictable variations; unique passwords and MFA are more important.

What should I do after clicking a phishing link?

If you entered credentials, change the password immediately from a trusted device and change it anywhere else it was reused. Revoke sessions, review MFA and recovery settings, scan the device, and contact the affected financial or work service if sensitive information was involved.
