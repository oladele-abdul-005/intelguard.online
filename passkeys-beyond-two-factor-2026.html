Two-Factor Isn’t Enough Anymore. Here’s What’s Next.

Tomas Berg · September 19, 2026 · 11 min read

Two-factor authentication was the single best security upgrade of the last decade, and it is still far better than nothing. But the attack that defeats it is now cheap, automated, and available as a service: a phishing page that relays your code to the real site in real time while you watch a convincing login screen. Passkeys close that gap by making the credential itself impossible to hand over. Here is how to move without locking yourself out of anything important.

Why codes stopped being enough

A one-time code is a secret you can be persuaded to type somewhere else. Modern phishing kits sit between you and the genuine service: you enter your username, password, and six-digit code on the attacker's page, the kit forwards each of them to the real site within seconds, and the resulting session cookie is captured. You end up logged in, everything looks normal, and the attacker has a valid session that often survives a password change.

SMS has extra problems on top of this — SIM swap fraud and message interception — but the underlying issue applies to authenticator apps too. Push notification approvals added a different weakness: prompt fatigue, where a user under a barrage of requests eventually taps accept. Number matching helped, but the pattern remains that a human is asked to make a security decision under pressure.

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is a cryptographic key pair. The private half stays on your device or in your password manager and never leaves it; the public half sits with the service. Signing in means your device proves it holds the private key, usually after a fingerprint, face check, or PIN. There is no shared secret to type, which is why there is nothing useful for a phishing page to capture.

The second protection is domain binding. A passkey created for your bank will simply not be offered on a lookalike domain, because the browser checks the origin before the key is used. That removes the judgement call that catches out even careful people: you no longer have to spot the misspelled address, because the credential refuses to appear.

The short version: a password is something you can be tricked into revealing, a code is something you can be tricked into relaying, and a passkey is neither. It is the first mainstream authentication method that does not depend on the user spotting the fraud.

Where passkeys are in 2026

Support is broad among large platforms, major banks in several markets, retailers, and developer tools, and all mainstream browsers and operating systems handle the flow. The rough edges have moved from “does it work” to “where does it live”: passkeys sync through an ecosystem account or a password manager, and moving between ecosystems still varies in smoothness.

Coverage is genuinely uneven below the top tier. Plenty of mid-sized services, government portals, utilities, and smaller banks still offer only passwords plus SMS. Some services add passkeys as an extra factor rather than a replacement, which delivers a fraction of the benefit. And a common failure remains: a service with perfect passkey support that will still email a reset link to a compromised mailbox, leaving the weakest path wide open.

A migration order that avoids lockouts

  • Start with the email account that receives password resets. Everything else depends on it.
  • Then the password manager or ecosystem account where the passkeys themselves will live.
  • Then money: banking, payments, brokerage, and anything storing a card.
  • Then identity: government portals, mobile carrier, and domain registrar — the accounts used to take over others.
  • Then everyday services, in whatever order you meet them.

At each step, register at least two passkeys or keep a proven backup method before removing the old one. Print or securely store recovery codes offline. The genuine risk in this transition is not attack; it is a person with a single passkey on a single phone that falls in a river.

Hardware keys still have a role

For administrators, journalists, executives, and anyone with elevated access, a pair of hardware security keys remains the strongest practical option. They are unphishable, independent of any cloud account, and they work when a phone is lost or compromised. Buy two, register both, and keep one somewhere physically separate — a safe, an office drawer, a relative's house.

For everyone else, synced passkeys in a reputable manager or platform account are the right trade-off. The convenience is what makes them stick, and an authentication method people abandon after a week protects nobody. Reserve hardware keys for the accounts whose compromise would be catastrophic.

Fix recovery, or none of this matters

Attackers increasingly skip the front door and target recovery: a support call with a few personal details, a carrier account takeover to intercept SMS, or an old secondary email nobody monitors. Audit every recovery route on your important accounts. Remove stale phone numbers and addresses, set a carrier port-out PIN, and replace guessable security questions with random strings stored in your password manager.

Then check the sessions. Most services list active logins and connected applications; review them quarterly and revoke anything unrecognised or unused. Signing out everywhere is also the correct first response to a suspected compromise, because a stolen session cookie survives a password change but not a global sign-out.

Regional notes

United States

Financial institutions vary widely: some offer passkeys, many still rely on SMS. Set a port-out PIN with your mobile carrier to reduce SIM swap risk, and check whether your bank supports app-based approval as an interim improvement over text messages.

Canada

Major banks and government services are adopting stronger authentication at different speeds, and bilingual support pages sometimes lag behind feature releases. Confirm what recovery looks like in practice before you remove an older method, particularly for accounts tied to provincial services.

United Kingdom

Strong customer authentication is well established for payments, and passkey support is expanding among banks and retailers. Be alert to voice phishing that claims to come from your bank's fraud team; a passkey protects the login, not a phone call that persuades you to move money yourself.

Australia

After several high-profile breaches, identity verification and recovery have tightened across many services, which is good for security and occasionally painful for access. Keep identity documents and recovery details current, and register a second passkey before travelling somewhere with poor connectivity.

Questions readers ask

Are passkeys really safer than an authenticator app?

Yes, for phishing specifically. An authenticator code can be relayed to the real site by a fake page in real time; a passkey cannot, because the private key never leaves your device and the browser will not offer it on the wrong domain.

What happens if I lose the device holding my passkeys?

If your passkeys sync through a platform account or password manager, you restore access by signing in on a new device. That is why you should register a second passkey or keep offline recovery codes before removing older sign-in methods.

Should I delete my password once I add a passkey?

Only when the service supports passkey-only sign-in and you have a tested second passkey or recovery route. Until then, keep a long unique password in a manager as a fallback and treat the passkey as the primary method.

Do I still need two-factor authentication anywhere?

Yes. Many services do not support passkeys yet, so keep app-based two-factor codes on those accounts and move away from SMS where you can. Two-factor remains far better than a password alone.

The bottom line

Passkeys are the first authentication upgrade that removes the user's ability to be tricked, and in 2026 they are ready for the accounts that matter most. Move email first, then your password manager, then money and identity accounts. Register two credentials everywhere, clean up recovery routes and old sessions, and keep app-based codes on the services that have not caught up yet.
