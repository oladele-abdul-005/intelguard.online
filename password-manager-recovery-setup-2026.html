A password manager is one password away from locking you out

Tomas Berg · October 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Password managers are the rare security product that mostly delivers. They make unique passwords easy, they refuse to autofill on the wrong domain, and they turn a breach at one service into a dull afternoon instead of a chain reaction across your life. The trade is that you now hold one credential worth more than all the others combined, protected by a recovery process you have probably never tested. The horror stories are rarely about the encryption. They are about a person, a lost phone, and a recovery kit that was never printed.

Practical principle: the vault is only as good as your ability to open it on a machine you have never used before.

How the vault protects you, briefly

Every serious password manager works the same way. Your master password is stretched by a key-derivation function, something like PBKDF2 with a high iteration count or Argon2id, and the result unlocks a locally decrypted copy of your vault. The provider stores an encrypted blob it cannot read. Some products add a second secret held only on your devices, which is why losing every device and the printed recovery sheet at once is genuinely bad.

The consequence is not a bug, it is the design: nobody at the company can reset your master password and hand your vault back. That is exactly what makes the product trustworthy. It is also what makes the recovery plan the most important part of the setup, and the part almost everyone skips.

Build the master passphrase before you import anything

Length beats cleverness. Four or five randomly chosen words, at least sixteen characters, used nowhere else, will beat a short string of symbols that you have to write on a sticky note to remember. Generate the words rather than inventing them, because invented phrases follow patterns that guessing tools already try.

Practise typing it until it is boring. Ten repetitions, then five more the next day. A passphrase you can type from muscle memory at an airport is worth more than one you have to look up in a place where looking it up defeats the purpose.

Then decide, in advance, where the written copy lives. Not in the vault, obviously. Not in a note on the same phone that unlocks the vault. A sealed envelope in a safe, a fireproof box, or a second address you can actually reach is a reasonable answer. So is a trusted person who knows where to find it and why it matters.

Day one, in order

The sequence matters more than it looks. Do the recovery work before you move a single password in, so that a mistake costs you nothing.

  • Create the account on a computer. Setting up a vault only on a phone makes the first recovery drill much harder than it needs to be.
  • Print or export the recovery kit. Products call it different things: an Emergency Kit, a secret key, a recovery code. If it exists, save it before anything else, and keep it where you keep documents you cannot replace.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication immediately. Use an authenticator app or a hardware security key. Register a second key if the product supports more than one, and store it somewhere other than your bag.
  • Save the two-factor recovery codes outside the vault. A recovery code for the vault, stored inside the vault, is a joke that writes itself.
  • Import from your browser, then delete the export. The CSV that browsers produce is plain text containing every password you own. Delete it properly once the import is confirmed, and check the download folder and any cloud sync that may have picked it up.
  • Turn on autofill and passkey storage. The everyday security benefit comes from the manager filling the right site automatically, not from you copying passwords by hand.
  • Set up emergency access if it is offered. Trusted-contact features usually involve a waiting period measured in days, which is a good balance between being locked out and being too easy to reach.
  • Run the drill. Open a private browsing window on a computer you have not used, and sign in using only the recovery kit, as though your phone were at the bottom of a lake. If anything is missing, you have found it at the best possible moment.

The recovery kit is the whole plan

This is where the products differ most, and where marketing pages are least useful. Some managers pair your password with a long secret key that is printed in the recovery document, which means a decent master password plus the document is enough to get back in from a new machine. Others offer emergency access that lets a nominated contact request the vault after a delay you configure. Others have no recovery at all beyond knowing the password.

Read your own product's page on recovery while you are calm, not while you are locked out. Write down three things on paper: the email address the account uses, what the recovery process requires, and where the recovery document is kept. That piece of paper is the actual plan.

Two-factor, done properly

SMS codes are the weakest option and the one most worth replacing, because a phone number can be moved to another device by someone who convinces a carrier. An authenticator app is a solid middle ground, and a hardware security key is the strongest widely available choice. Whichever you pick, the rule is the same: the vault's own second factor must live outside the vault. Otherwise a single unlocked session hands over everything.

For daily convenience, storing one-time codes for ordinary accounts inside the vault is a reasonable trade. For email, banking, and the vault itself, keep the codes elsewhere. Be strict about the few accounts that can reset all the others, and relaxed about the rest.

What breaks the model in practice

Very few vaults are compromised by someone guessing a master password. The realistic failures are duller. Infostealer malware lifts an already-unlocked session from a browser on an infected machine. A plain-text export lingers in a downloads folder for a year. A recovery code ends up screenshotted into a photo library that syncs to a shared account. A household shares one vault login, so nobody can rotate anything without breaking someone else's day. Two-factor is enabled on the vault and never tested, so nobody discovers the old phone number is still the registered factor.

Each of those has a cheap fix, and none of them involve changing products. Run an update on the computer, stop keeping exports, give each adult their own vault, and test the second factor once every few months.

Passkeys change the shape, not the need

Passkeys are a genuine improvement: they are bound to the site, so they cannot be phished, and most managers now store and sync them. But a passkey still lives somewhere, and that somewhere can be lost. Keep a second sign-in method on the accounts that matter, do not delete every recovery option because the first passkey sign-in worked, and make sure the device sync you rely on is actually switched on.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

The encryption model is the same everywhere; what changes is the paperwork around it. In the United States, state breach-notification laws mean a provider that suffers an incident must tell you, and consumer-protection rules apply to how it describes its security. In Canada, federal privacy law covers how a commercial service handles your data, and provincial rules can add requirements. In the UK and the EU, data-protection law gives you rights over the account data even though it cannot give you the encrypted contents back. In Australia, the Privacy Act applies to the provider, and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme sets out when you must be contacted.

For work credentials, the deciding factor is usually your employer's policy rather than the law. If your organisation supplies a vault, do not mix personal and work secrets inside it, and read the acceptable-use policy before storing client material in a personal account.

The bottom line

Use a password manager. The alternative, which is reusing a handful of passwords you can remember, is worse in every scenario that actually happens. Then spend twenty extra minutes doing the boring part: a long passphrase, a printed recovery kit in a place you can reach, two hardware keys or an authenticator app, recovery codes stored outside the vault, and one rehearsal on a clean browser. That twenty minutes is the difference between a tool that protects you and a single point of failure you have forgotten about.

Questions readers ask

What happens if I forget my master password?

With a zero-knowledge manager, the provider cannot reset it and restore your vault. Recovery depends on what you set up in advance: a printed recovery kit or secret key, emergency access granted to a trusted contact, or device-based recovery. If none of those exist, the encrypted data is usually unrecoverable by design. That is why the recovery document should be produced on day one.

Is it safe to store two-factor codes inside the password manager?

For ordinary accounts it is a reasonable convenience, and the codes are protected by the same encryption as everything else. The exception is the vault itself and the email account that can reset it. Keep those second factors in a separate app or a hardware key, and keep recovery codes for them offline, so one unlocked session cannot hand over everything.

Do I need to pay for a password manager?

Free tiers are genuinely usable and much better than reuse. Paid plans usually add cross-device sync, emergency access, hardware-key support, and file storage. Choose on recovery features rather than on storage limits: the question that matters is how you get back in when your only device is gone, and whether that process has been tested.
