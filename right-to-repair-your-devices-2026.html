Right to repair in practice: how to keep your devices alive longer

Priya Menon · October 11, 2026 · 9 min read

Most devices do not die. They get abandoned. A laptop with a tired battery, a phone with a cracked display, a tablet that quietly stopped receiving updates — each one is a decision point, and for years the default answer was to replace rather than repair. That default is shifting. Repair scores, spare-part supply, and clearer documentation have made it easier to keep hardware alive, but the practical work still happens at the kitchen table: working out whether a fix is safe, affordable, and genuinely worth your Saturday.

Practical principle: repair is a calculation, not a moral position. Safety, cost, data risk, and remaining software support decide whether a fix is worth doing — not loyalty to a device.

Start with the device you almost replaced

Before shopping, audit what you already own. The most common repairable problems are boring ones: a battery that no longer lasts a day, a port packed with lint, a fan clogged with dust, a display cable that loosened after a drop, or thermal paste that dried out years ago. Several of these cost nothing but an hour and a screwdriver.

Write down what is actually wrong. “Slow” is not a diagnosis. Is the device throttling because it is hot? Is storage full? Is a background sync process pegging the disk? A five-minute check in the system monitor or battery health screen will tell you whether you have a hardware problem, a software problem, or a device that is simply old for the job you are asking it to do.

Check repairability before the next purchase

The cheapest repair is the one you planned for. When you buy, look at what the manufacturer publishes: service manuals, official spare parts, battery replacement pricing, and how many years of security updates the device will receive. A laptop with a documented teardown and a user-replaceable battery will outlive an identically priced machine that is glued shut.

Public repair scores and independent teardown sites are useful shortcuts, but read them with care. A score reflects the device at launch. What matters for you is the current situation: can you still buy the part, does the shop in your city service this model, and does the repair require software authorisation that only the manufacturer can grant?

Batteries are the repair that usually pays

Battery degradation is the single most common reason good hardware gets retired. A phone or laptop battery is a consumable, and after two or three years a replacement often restores most of the original experience for a fraction of the price of a new device.

Two safety rules matter more than any others. First, never pry at a swollen battery — a puffed cell can catch fire if it is punctured, so take it to a professional or a designated battery drop-off. Second, use a quality replacement. Cheap unbranded cells often lie about capacity and can fail early. Where the manufacturer sells the part directly, the modest premium is usually worth it.

Watch for parts pairing and software locks

Modern hardware increasingly ties components to the device in software. Replace a display, a battery, or a camera and the system may warn about an “unverified part,” disable a feature such as battery health reporting, or refuse to calibrate until an authorised tool approves the swap. This is why the same repair can be easy in one model and impractical in another.

Before you buy a part, search for the specific model plus the part name and read what owners report. If a repair requires dealer software, budget for a shop instead of attempting it yourself. If a device makes independent repair structurally impossible, treat that as a purchasing signal — it tells you what the next five years will cost.

Tools, manuals, and the first-repair tax

A basic kit covers most jobs: precision screwdrivers, plastic pry tools, tweezers, a suction cup, and adhesive strips for reassembly. Add a magnetic mat or a few small cups for screws, because losing one tiny fastener is the classic way a simple repair turns into a two-week project. Anti-static precautions are worth the small effort on anything with exposed boards.

Follow a written or video guide for your exact model rather than a generic one. Disconnect the battery before touching connectors, photograph each stage as you go, and keep the fasteners with the step they belong to. The first repair is always the slowest; by the third, most people have a rhythm and a box of spares that makes future fixes trivial.

Know when to stop repairing

Repair stops making sense in a few clear cases. If the device no longer receives security updates, repairing it means extending a machine you should not use for banking, email, or work. If the quote is approaching half the price of a comparable replacement, the money is better spent elsewhere. If the fault involves a swollen battery, a cracked screen on a device that is also water-damaged, or a sealed high-voltage assembly, the risk is not worth the saving.

When you do retire a device, retire it properly: back up your data, sign out of accounts, and factory reset it. A repaired or refurbished device that still holds your session tokens is a security problem, not a bargain.

Regional notes for the US, Canada, UK, and Australia

In the United States, right-to-repair rules vary by state, and some manufacturers have responded by widening parts and manual availability. Independent repair shops are widespread, so getting a quote before deciding is usually quick.

In Canada, cold winters make batteries and displays more failure-prone, and mail-in repair times can stretch in remote areas. Provincial consumer protection rules and the availability of authorised service centres are worth checking before a big repair.

In the United Kingdom, repairability and spare-parts availability are increasingly published at the point of sale, and repair businesses benefit from clear consumer rights. Ask for a written quote and confirm whether the repair affects any remaining manufacturer warranty.

In Australia, distance and storm-season power problems shorten the life of batteries and power supplies. Consumer guarantees give buyers meaningful remedies for faults that are not their doing, so keep receipts and record serial numbers.

A right-to-repair checklist

  • Diagnose before you buy: heat, storage, and software problems are not hardware faults.
  • Check battery health first — it is the repair that most often pays for itself.
  • Buy the part by exact model number, and prefer official or well-reviewed suppliers.
  • Verify whether the part needs software authorisation before you start.
  • Never pry a swollen battery; take it to a professional or a drop-off point.
  • Disconnect the battery, photograph each step, and keep screws with their step.
  • Stop when updates have ended, the quote nears half a replacement, or the repair is unsafe.

The bottom line

Right to repair is mostly a habit: diagnose honestly, buy devices you can fix, treat the battery as a consumable, and know the point at which a repair stops making sense. The goal is not to keep every gadget forever. It is to stop replacing good hardware for want of a battery, a port, or a screwdriver.

Questions readers ask

Is it safe to replace a phone battery at home?

On many models, yes, if the manufacturer or a reputable guide documents the process and the battery is not swollen or punctured. A swollen battery should be handled by a repair shop or a designated drop-off point rather than pried out at home.

What is parts pairing and why does it matter?

Parts pairing is software that ties a component, such as a display or battery, to the specific device it shipped with. A genuine replacement part may need authorisation before features work, which affects both DIY repairs and independent shops.

When is repairing a device a bad idea?

Skip the repair when the device no longer receives security updates, when the cost approaches half the price of a replacement, when the repair involves a swollen battery or a sealed high-voltage assembly, or when your data cannot be safely wiped and backed up.
