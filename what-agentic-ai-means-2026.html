What “Agentic” Actually Means, Explained Simply

Dara Okafor · September 20, 2026 · 9 min read

“Agentic” has become the word vendors reach for when a product does slightly more than answer a question. That vagueness is expensive, because the difference between a tool that drafts text and a tool that clicks buttons in your accounting system is the difference between a typo and a payment. This is a plain definition, a way to tell the categories apart, and the questions worth asking before you let any of it near real work.

A definition that holds up

A system is agentic to the degree that it pursues a goal over multiple steps, decides for itself what to do next, uses tools or takes actions in other systems, and adapts when a step fails. Notice that this is a spectrum rather than a category. Almost every real product sits somewhere on it, and the useful question is not “is this an agent” but “how much of the loop runs without a human”.

The four ingredients are worth separating because products mix them differently. Goal orientation means you state an outcome, not a sequence. Autonomy means it chooses the next step. Tool use means it can affect systems beyond the chat window. Recovery means it notices a failure and tries something else rather than stopping or confidently inventing a result.

The four rungs of the ladder

  • Assistant. Answers, drafts, summarises. You take every action. Risk: wrong information.
  • Tool user. Searches, reads a document, runs a calculation, calls an API when asked. You still approve each step. Risk: wrong source, wrong call.
  • Workflow agent. Executes a defined multi-step process with permission to act in named systems. Risk: wrong action taken repeatedly.
  • Open-ended agent. Given a broad goal and a wide set of permissions, it decides the whole approach. Risk: compounding errors nobody sees until the end.

Most valuable deployments in 2026 sit on rungs two and three. The fourth is where the demonstrations are impressive and the production incidents come from, because error rates multiply across steps: a system that is 95 percent reliable per action is about 60 percent reliable across ten unsupervised actions.

Why the demos look better than the reality

Demonstrations are run on cooperative websites, clean data, and tasks chosen because they work. Real environments include expired sessions, pop-ups, rate limits, ambiguous records, duplicate customers, and colleagues who named a folder something unhelpful in 2019. Agents deal with novelty poorly and rarely say “I am not sure” unless the product has been explicitly designed to make stopping an acceptable outcome.

The other gap is verification. A human doing a ten-step task notices when step four looks odd. An agent typically proceeds, and the cost of checking its work can approach the cost of doing the work. Agents pay off where verification is cheap and fast — a build that passes tests, a reconciliation that balances, a draft you can scan in ten seconds — and struggle where checking is as slow as doing.

The single best predictor of whether an agent will save time: can you verify the result in a fraction of the time it took to produce? If not, you have moved the work rather than removed it.

Where agents genuinely earn their keep

Repetitive, well-bounded, reversible tasks with clear success criteria. Triaging and labelling incoming requests. Gathering information from several sources into a structured summary. Drafting code changes that a test suite can validate. Reconciling records between two systems and flagging the exceptions. Monitoring something continuously and escalating the unusual cases to a person.

The common pattern is that the agent does the tedious breadth and a human makes the consequential decision. That division is not a temporary limitation while the technology improves — it is a sensible design for anything that touches money, customers, or permanent records, because accountability does not automate.

Questions to ask a vendor

  • What exactly can it do without me? Ask for the list of actions and the systems they touch.
  • What permissions does it need, and can they be narrowed to specific accounts, folders, or spending limits?
  • Where is the audit log, and does it record the reasoning as well as the actions?
  • How do I stop it mid-task, and what state is left behind if I do?
  • What is undoable? Sent messages, payments, and deletions usually are not.
  • What does it do when uncertain — guess, stop, or ask?

A vendor that answers these crisply has thought about production. One that redirects to capability demonstrations has not. The answers also tell you what a pilot should measure, which is rarely raw capability and almost always the failure behaviour.

Deploying without drama

Start read-only. Let the agent observe and propose for a couple of weeks while a person executes, and log every disagreement. That produces a real accuracy baseline in your environment rather than a vendor benchmark. Then grant write access to one low-stakes, reversible task, with a spending or volume cap and a clear owner responsible for reviewing the log weekly.

Keep identity separate. Give the agent its own account with its own scoped credentials rather than borrowing a person's session, so its actions are distinguishable in logs and its access can be revoked without disrupting anyone. Review the permissions quarterly; agent access tends to accumulate quietly, and an over-permissioned automation is a security problem long before it is an AI problem.

Regional notes

United States

Sector rules matter more than general AI rules for most deployments — financial, healthcare, and employment contexts carry existing obligations that apply regardless of whether a human or an agent acted. Keep the audit trail that lets you demonstrate who authorised what.

Canada

Privacy expectations around automated processing of personal information are tightening, and bilingual service obligations can apply in customer-facing workflows. Confirm where the agent's provider processes data before connecting it to customer records.

United Kingdom

Existing data protection law already covers automated decisions with significant effects on individuals, so document meaningful human involvement in anything customer-affecting. Regulators have signalled a sector-led approach, which means your industry body's guidance is the practical reference point.

Australia

Guidance for automated decision-making in government and regulated sectors is developing, and organisations are expected to document oversight. For smaller businesses, the practical priorities are data location, clear audit trails, and not granting an agent payment authority without a cap and a human approval step.

Questions readers ask

What is the difference between an AI agent and a chatbot?

A chatbot produces text in response to a prompt. An agent pursues a goal over multiple steps, chooses its next action, uses tools or acts in other systems, and adapts when something fails. Most products sit somewhere between the two.

Are AI agents reliable enough for real work?

For bounded, reversible, easily verified tasks, often yes. For long unsupervised chains, reliability degrades quickly because per-step error rates compound. Keep humans on the consequential decisions and let agents handle the tedious breadth.

What permissions should an AI agent have?

The narrowest set that completes the task, granted to its own dedicated account rather than a person's session, with spending or volume caps, a full audit log, and a way to stop it mid-task. Review the permissions quarterly.

How do I pilot an agent safely?

Run it read-only for a couple of weeks so it proposes while a human executes, and log every disagreement to get a real accuracy baseline. Then allow write access to one low-stakes reversible task with a cap and a named owner.

The bottom line

Agentic simply means the software decides and acts over multiple steps rather than waiting for instructions each time. Treat it as a dial, not a badge: know how far along it your tool sits, insist on scoped permissions and real audit logs, deploy where verification is cheap, and keep a person accountable for anything irreversible.
